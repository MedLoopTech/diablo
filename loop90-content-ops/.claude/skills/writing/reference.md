# Writing — Reference

## Openings

The first two sentences decide whether the rest is read.

| Pattern | Example |
|---------|---------|
| The reader's own observation | "You've noticed it. Biryani sends your number up. Daal chawal doesn't — not the same way." |
| The specific contrast | "Same rice. Two meals. Two very different numbers." |
| The reframe | "The problem was never the roti. It was what was — and wasn't — next to it." |
| The direct question answered | "Can you eat rice with diabetes? Yes. Here's how the meal changes the answer." |

**Never open with:** "In today's fast-paced world", "Diabetes is a growing epidemic", a definition, or a statistic that isn't the point of the piece.

## Explaining mechanism

Two or three sentences. Then move to what it means.

**Too long:**
> Glycaemic index is a measure developed in 1981 that ranks carbohydrate-containing foods on a scale of 0 to 100 according to how much they raise blood glucose relative to a reference food, typically glucose or white bread, measured over a two-hour period in healthy volunteers…

**Right:**
> Glycaemic index tells you how fast a food raises your sugar when eaten alone. Glycaemic load adds the part that matters more day to day: how much of it you actually ate. But in a real meal, a third thing matters — what's on the plate alongside it.

## The "try this today" section

- One action, not three
- Specific enough to do without deciding anything
- Realistic for the persona's constraints

| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| "Eat more fibre" | "Add kachumber to tonight's rice — that's it" |
| "Exercise regularly" | "Walk for 10 minutes after dinner tonight" |
| "Monitor your levels" | "Log tomorrow's fasting reading before you eat anything" |

## Handling the medication boundary

When the topic brushes medication, route without coldness:

> As your numbers improve, your doctor may adjust your medication. That's their call, made with your full history in front of them — never something to change on your own.

Never: "you may be able to reduce your dose", "many patients come off metformin", or any timeline.

## Desi food vocabulary

Use: roti, chapati, paratha, chawal, biryani, pulao, daal, sabzi, kachumber, raita, lassi, chai, nihari, haleem, samosa, jalebi, mithai, suhoor, iftar.

Avoid as defaults: quinoa, kale, overnight oats, smoothie bowls, avocado toast, cauliflower rice.

If a Western food is genuinely the clearest example, use it — but never as the *default* frame.

## Code-switching (patient persona only)

Natural Roman Urdu in patient content: *thoda sa, bilkul, aaj se shuru, koi baat nahi, zaroor, halka.*

Rules:
- Never in doctor, pharma, or coach-recruitment content
- Never mid-clinical-instruction — thresholds and safety copy stay in clear English
- Sprinkle, don't saturate — it should read as natural speech, not performance

## Length discipline

If you're over the template's range, you've included something the brief didn't ask for. Cut that, not the takeaway.

## What "flagged_for_editing" is for

Use it honestly:

- "Para 4 mechanism may be too technical for grade 8"
- "Not sure the biryani example lands without a photo"
- "Opening might be too abrupt for a first-time reader"

Editing can fix what you flag. It can't fix what you hide.
